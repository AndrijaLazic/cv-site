At DigitalLeather I worked on two closely related FMEA products: FMEA Traditional and FMEA Excellence. Both are built around the same practical goal: helping automotive teams manage risk in a structured way, without forcing them to live inside scattered spreadsheets and disconnected documents.

The important difference is scope. FMEA Traditional focuses on the standard FMEA workflow and document-heavy parts of the process. FMEA Excellence is the more advanced product, where the AI, machine learning, prediction, optimization, and AI-assisted translation features live.

<ContentBlock title="FMEA Traditional">
  FMEA Traditional was centered on bringing familiar FMEA work into a more reliable application flow.

Many teams already had existing Excel-based documents, custom layouts, and internal templates. One of my responsibilities was building backend workflows that could support file import and export, including exports into custom templates. That mattered because adoption depends on meeting users where they already are.

The technical work was mostly about reliability and structure: parsing user files, keeping data consistent, generating documents that matched expected formats, and making those workflows practical for real client usage.

<ContentBlock title="FMEA Excellence">
  FMEA Excellence is the advanced risk-management platform I worked on as a software engineer in a small team. I took ownership of backend architecture and led development of core services in Python, while also contributing to Spring Boot, MySQL, Docker-based environments, and product-facing features.

This is where the AI-related work belongs. The product uses historical data, real-time data, and user-defined parameters to help predict future risk costs, forecast the value of action plans, and recommend better decisions for teams working with FMEA in automotive industry use cases.

<ContentBlock title="Prediction and decision support">
  The main AI workflow helps users compare risks, actions, costs, and expected returns. Instead of manually evaluating many possible actions, managers can see estimated ROI, expected return time, and projected scrap trends for a selected production process.

Behind the interface, the system combines multiple prediction steps. The models use station-level scrap data to estimate cost and forecast future trends, then optimization techniques help choose the best set of actions under the user's budget, time, and business constraints.

  <Callout type="tip" title="What made this valuable">
    The goal was not to make AI visible for its own sake. The value was putting prediction directly inside the decision-making workflow, where it could reduce manual analysis and make risk planning faster.
  </Callout>
</ContentBlock>

<ContentBlock title="AI assistant and translation">
  I also worked on AI-assisted features that supported FMEA knowledge and multilingual documentation.

The assistant side used a RAG-style workflow to answer practical questions about the application and the FMEA methodology. The translation side helped make business documentation easier to use across languages, which is important when clients have facilities, teams, and customers in different countries.

These features were useful because FMEA can be complex even for experienced teams. Good AI support should reduce friction, explain domain concepts, and help users keep moving through the workflow without taking ownership away from them.
